PACKAGE AAN-F-007 · THE ROLE ROOM

A uniform is read before its wearer speaks.

It can announce a role, gather a group, trigger an expectation and place a body inside an institution. Competence, obedience, aggression and trust grow out of relationships, so cloth alone cannot make them. The evidence is also narrower than the symbol.

By RN Collins · Neuroscience, fashion and social perception · September 2026

Education, not medical advice.

BODY GARMENT ROLE INSTITUTION OBSERVER

READ THE SYSTEM

Silhouette is only the first instruction.

Cut, color, insignia, fit and repetition help a uniform become recognizable. Law, hierarchy, training, labor, media and lived encounters supply its force. A uniform can be an earned marker, an imposed dress code, a source of pride, a target, an access device, or several at once.

01

The observer does not see cloth alone

Person perception combines dress with face, body, setting and what the observer already believes. A uniform can accelerate inferences about occupation, status, competence, warmth or authority. Inference is not verification.

The same insignia can mean safety to one observer and danger to another. Prior encounters, community history, racialization, gender, disability, age and place do not sit outside perception; they shape it.

LEGIBILITY

A role becomes quickly readable.

Fast recognition can coordinate a hospital, station, kitchen or runway. It can also compress a person into a category.

AUTHORITY

The garment borrows institutional power.

Law, organization and public recognition confer authority. Navy wool and a peaked cap only carry the signal.

BELONGING

Similarity can gather people and divide them.

Shared dress may support identification while enforcing boundaries around who belongs, who serves and who is watched.

02

For the wearer, a role can become salient

Experiments have asked whether wearing role-marked clothing shifts attention, moral decisions or self-related processing. The results are specific to each study. None amounts to a general law.

In one three-experiment study, Canadian students, who were not police officers, wore a police-style uniform. Reaction-time tasks suggested attentional bias toward low-status targets represented by hoodies. They did not show that bias toward Black faces. A later laboratory study compared military, Red Cross and civilian dress during an unusual moral-choice task. Neither study shows how professionals behave in real institutions.

A laboratory role cue is not a profession, and a measured response is not a destiny.

Civile & Obhi (2017) ↗ · Pech & Caspar (2023) ↗

03

A brain image does not reveal a uniform center

A small fMRI study showed participants photographs of themselves and others in professional uniform or casual clothing during adjective judgments. The reported activity was distributed across regions implicated in self, social and affective processing. That is a task-specific pattern. It does not prove that a uniform installs professional pride or rewires a personality.

Hong and colleagues (2019) ↗

04

The institution travels with the clothes

Uniforms mark professional boundaries because organizations decide who wears what, where and with which powers. A UK hospital case study showed that replacing profession-specific dress with a corporate uniform was experienced as a change in status and identity. The case shows how standardization rearranges recognition. It says little about whether all healthcare workers need one look.

Fashion must therefore ask more than whether a uniform is beautiful. Who chose it? Who must wear it? Does it fit varied bodies and religious practice? Who launders and replaces it? What labor, danger or discipline becomes visible, and what disappears?

Timmons & East (2011) ↗

CLAIM LEDGER · UNIFORM AND SOCIAL-PERCEPTION BOUNDARIES

THE FITTED CLAIM

Uniforms can organize perception and make roles salient. They do not contain a fixed psychology.

Supported

Dress contributes to person perception; uniforms can mark role, status and professional boundaries.

Narrow evidence

Specific uniforms changed specific attention, moral-choice or self-judgment measures in particular studies.

Not established

Automatic obedience, competence, aggression, belonging, trust, or one universal neural response.
