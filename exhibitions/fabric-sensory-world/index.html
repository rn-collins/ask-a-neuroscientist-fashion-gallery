PACKAGE AAN-F-003 · EVIDENCE EDITION

Your skin does not read the fiber label.

Why one fabric can feel settling today and unbearable tomorrow, and why the answer lives in an encounter among textile, garment, body and world.

By RN Collins · Neuroscience, fashion and sensory autonomy · September 2026

Education, not medical advice.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Your nervous system builds the feel of a fabric. One material name cannot hold it. Receptors encode deformation, vibration, temperature, movement and potential harm; the brain interprets those signals alongside expectation, attention, prior experience and present conditions.

01

Touch is an ongoing conversation

A sleeve is never simply “soft.” It has fibers, yarn, construction, finish, seams and weight. It meets a particular patch of skin with a particular pressure, temperature, speed and duration. Then movement keeps rewriting the contact.

Fast, myelinated Aβ afferents help the nervous system discriminate pressure, vibration, edges and texture. Unmyelinated C-tactile afferents contribute to the affective quality of gentle moving touch on hairy skin. Pain and itch pathways add other messages. These systems interact; none is a universal comfort meter.

A fiber label names ingredients. It does not predict an experience.

Mechanism review: Liljencrantz & Olausson, Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience (2014). Open article and figures ↗

TEXTILE ≠ GARMENT

Cotton can still scrape, trap, pull or soothe.

“Cotton” leaves out yarn twist, weave, surface finish, laundering, moisture and every construction detail. A seam can concentrate force. A label can repeat the same stimulus hundreds of times. A loose garment can flutter; a fitted one can maintain pressure. The same shirt changes after sweat, heat, washing or a long day.

A 2024 textile-touch study found that measured tactile sensitivity influenced how participants rated textile textures. Its task used hand touch in a laboratory, which differs from full-body wear. Even so, it undercuts the idea that everyone meets one objective softness.

Mehta et al., “Tactile sensitivity alters textile touch perception” (2024). Open access ↗

02

Calming is not a property you can sew into a tag

Steady pressure, predictable contact, low friction or familiar weight may feel organizing to one person. The same pressure may feel entrapping, painful or too hot to another. Small studies of compression and sensory garments do not support universal therapeutic claims.

Context also changes touch. Expectation and attention can alter pleasantness; an acceptable seam at home may become impossible under fluorescent light, time pressure and crowded transit. “Today” is a real variable. It does not mean the person is inconsistent.

Evidence boundary: Lawson et al. (2022) and Grandits et al. (2023). Sensory-garment study ↗ · Compression study ↗

THE OBJECT LESSON

“Silk” cannot tell you how this dress meets a body.

The photograph records form and leaves out feel. It lets us inspect fitted construction, high neck, fringe, gathers, closures and layers. It cannot tell us the weave’s hand, the lining, the wearer’s temperature or the accumulated sensation after an hour.

This is why sensory fashion needs object records plus first-person wear records. Luxurious material names are a poor guide.

03

Autistic testimony is evidence

In a mixed-methods study of ten autistic adults, Kyriacou and colleagues documented tactile defensiveness, positive sensory experiences, restricted clothing choices and effects on identity and participation. Some participants described seams, labels or disliked fabrics as distressing; others sought particular sensations.

The finding is variability. It does not produce an “autism wardrobe.” Autism does not make every person prefer cotton, hate wool or benefit from compression. ADHD and autism are not interchangeable, and neither can be diagnosed from clothing preferences.

Kyriacou et al., Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders (2023). Canonical article ↗

ITCH · PAIN · DISTRESS

Unbearable is not an exaggeration.

Itch, pain and affective aversion are distinct experiences that may overlap. In tactile allodynia, normally innocuous moving contact, including fabric brushing skin, can produce pain. That clinical phenomenon should not be used to medicalize every strong dislike, nor should strong sensory distress be dismissed because there is no visible injury.

New, persistent or worsening pain, rash, numbness or burning belongs with a qualified clinician. This exhibition cannot determine whether a reaction comes from contact dermatitis, neuropathic pain, migraine, sensory sensitivity or something else.

04

Design from the variables

The practical unit is the whole encounter: surface, seam, pressure, temperature, moisture, movement, duration, body site, predictability and context. Change one variable at a time when possible. Turn a garment inside out, add a soft base layer, remove a label safely, compare standing with walking, or note when heat changes tolerability.

Accommodation is not failure. Repeating a safe outfit, buying duplicates, avoiding a texture or changing dress expectations can preserve attention for the life happening beyond the clothes.

THE EVIDENCE-FITTED ANSWER

Why can one fabric feel calming and another unbearable?

Because touch is produced by a nervous system encountering a constructed garment in context. Material properties matter, but so do seams, pressure, temperature, movement, body site, duration, expectation, prior experience, pain and individual sensitivity. No fiber is universally soothing or intolerable.

We can say

Touch uses multiple peripheral pathways; tactile sensitivity changes textile ratings; garment details and context shape experience.

We cannot say

A fabric diagnoses neurodivergence, compression treats everyone, or “natural” fibers guarantee comfort.

Still open

How lab texture ratings translate to hours of real wear across diverse bodies, cultures, climates and disabilities.
